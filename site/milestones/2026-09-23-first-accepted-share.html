Mujina's first pool-accepted share on BZM2 silicon

2026-09-23T02:37:09Z. Mujina, the open-source (GPL-3.0-or-later) Bitcoin mining software, submitted a share that a public pool accepted. It came from an RDS hashboard carrying 100 BZM2 ASICs, running on Mujina's own driver from job to result to submission.

At that second the chain tip was block 968,216 (000000000000000000014df86e986ca182365da99657cdfd1c22c3326d6a8cdc), so the share was mined toward block 968,217. Two independent block explorers, mempool.space and blockstream.info, agree on the tip's height, hash and header time. Header times are set by miners, and this tip was only 32 s old, so "tip at that second" is stated with that caveat. The pool accepting the share is the evidence that its job was on the current tip.

What ran

  • Hardware: one RDS hashboard, 100 BZM2 ASICs on a single 9-bit, 5 Mbaud chain.
  • Start-up: the unit's stock software powered and calibrated the board. Mujina then took the chain over without dropping power, reset every engine, ungated them, and read back that they were ready. It set a separate nonce range for each ASIC and began dispatching work from the pool.
  • Pool: OCEAN, stratum v1, difficulty 262,144, under a worker named mujina.

What it measured

Over a 610 s hold, from the first job to the pause:
shares accepted / rejected 13 / 0, at difficulty 262,144
hash rate (Mujina's share-based estimate) 26-29 TH/s, one board
trips, arms, scrams none

When the harness paused mining at the end, the load came off within a second, with Mujina still running. The engines stop when work stops.

Figures published by explicit decision (Ronald Bynoe, 2026-09-23): the share counts and window, and the hash rate. Power, efficiency and temperatures from this run are recorded in the project's case record and are not published.

What it took

A first dispatch, one run earlier, returned nothing. Every result was missing, and power stayed at idle. A verified investigation found three separate defects, and any one of them would have prevented an accepted share. Each fix carries a test that fails without it, and the first was confirmed on the silicon before any code was written:

  1. The engines were clock-gated. Mujina's take-over reset gates every engine's cores, and nothing ungated them. We read the engine's config register on the chip after the reset (gated), wrote the run value, and read it back (running). Mujina now ungates after every reset and refuses to dispatch unless the readback agrees.
  2. The job and the returned nonce were in the wrong byte order, and the nonce offset was wrong. Checked against a public captured hardware result: under the old rule it decodes to a header with 1 leading zero bit; under the new rule, 34.
  3. Every engine hashed identical work. The silicon does not split work between engines or ASICs, so without distinct work the same nonces were hashed 23,600 times over. Each ASIC now has its own nonce range, and each engine its own extranonce2.

The safety behaviour that surrounded those runs was exercised for real. A thermal interlock refuses work until every die has reported. A stall watch stops a chain that is given work but returns nothing. A ladder de-energises the board if that does not clear. All three fired correctly on the failed runs, including one caused by a sequencing mistake of ours, in which the sensor stream never came up. Each time, the board ended dark and safe.

What this does not claim

  • Not an efficiency comparison. No efficiency figure is published here, and none has yet been measured the same way against the stock software on the same board.
  • Not a cold start. The stock software powered the board. Mujina controlling the power supply from cold is separate work.
  • One board of three.
  • Not upstream yet. This runs on a development branch. The enablement goes to the upstream project as a reviewed change, once it is ready.
